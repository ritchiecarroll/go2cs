Census (Go side, go/types, red-tested on a planted package; reconciled with the
committed C#: +4% literals, +3.5% loops; three targets agree):
- `[1, 2, 3]` needs slice<T>'s foreach enumerator to yield T, not (nint, T).
  That breaks every `foreach (var (i, v) in s)` over a slice or array:
  6,041 in std (2,115 production), 349 in 189 behavioral dirs, about 200 in
  hand-owned files, about 20 in GolibTests. golib itself: 0 errors (spike;
  the same build fails on a planted pair loop).
- 81% of std's unnamed slice literals (11,423 of 14,035) sit where C# knows the
  target type and become `[...]`; `:=`, range operands, interface args, append and
  the like (2,612) keep `slice<T>([...])`. Maps have no C# 14 form.
- Consumers: `foreach (var (i, v) in s)` stops compiling (our own consumer doc and
  CSharpConsumer teach it); `foreach (var v in s)` silently gives values, not tuples.
  An assembly built against .2 fails at run time under .3 (missing GetEnumerator),
  and B4's range lets NuGet restore it, so .3 needs a nugetgo rebuild wave, as .2 did.

Questions for the owner:
4.1 pair loop: (a) `foreach (var (i, v) in range(s))`, index-only `range(len(s))`;
    (b) index-only keeps `range(s)`, pairs get a new name such as `pairs(s)`.
4.2 bare `[...]`: (a) every targeted position; (b) not in call arguments (955),
    which avoids C#'s ReadOnlySpan overload preference at a mixed look.
4.3 scope: (a) slices only, arrays keep their form (a builder cannot pad
    [5]int{1, 2}); (b) also named slice types later (745 literals, unsized).
Recommended: 4.1 (a), 4.2 (a), 4.3 (a).
